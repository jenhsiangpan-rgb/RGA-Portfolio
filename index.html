How an experience study works, and where my projects sit

An experience study compares the claims a life insurer actually paid with the claims its assumptions predicted. The team's process has three stages. Most of my work fell in the first and last. My manager's walkthrough sessions showed me how the middle stage connects to both.

One of the team's goals this year was to use more AI and automation to cut repetitive work and leave more time for higher-value analysis. Each of my projects reflects that goal.

Project 1 · Weeks 1–2

Data cleaning

From a one-week migration to a few hours

Each client study came with raw data, a target output to match, and roughly 1,000 lines of Python that cleaned the data. My job was to rebuild that logic as SQL in Snowflake.

Payout annuities pay income for life, so a study measures how long each life was at risk and whether the person died in the observation window. A joint-life contract produces two lives. The second life's exposure starts only after the first life dies and any guarantee period ends. Escalating pensions, deletion rules for invalid exposures, and risk banding by benefit size all had to be reproduced exactly.

I did the first migration by hand, which took about a week. Then I built a Claude skill in Snowflake that generalizes the work into the loop below and repeats it until every column matches the target output.

The hardest part

Some rules existed only in the data. I had to infer an undocumented, client-specific rule by sampling individual rows and working out the expected values by hand, then confirm the fix against every affected column.

Result

  • About a week to a few hoursto migrate a new client's study
  • Minutes to secondsto run a converted study, with no manual file handling
  • Matches the targeton every logic-driven column, with documentation for future users

What I learned

Translating code is the easy half. The real work is recovering the business rules hidden inside it, and a systematic validation loop is what makes that recoverable. I also learned to separate two kinds of time savings: the one-time effort of migrating and the recurring runtime of the pipeline.

Project 2 · Weeks 3–4

Results analysis

How much claim variation is just luck?

Actuaries need to know whether a month's claims are bad luck or a real shift in experience. The team already used Monte Carlo simulation to model expected variation, but running it depended on manual, fragmented Excel and SQL steps. My role was to optimize that workflow.

I rebuilt it as a single Snowflake notebook that runs the whole chain in one click. It simulates 1,000 scenarios across 21 time windows and four claim segments, builds the expected-versus-simulated reference table, computes actual-to-expected results (A/E), and plots them against bands at one and two standard deviations. The output supports a monthly financial issues meeting, and the notebook rolls forward to the latest complete month on its own.

How the simulation works

Each insured gets one probabilistic trial per scenario, with the expected claim probability as the chance of a claim. The claim amount counts only when the trial fires. Repeating this across many scenarios gives a distribution of plausible outcomes. Plotting actual results against that distribution shows at a glance whether a result is ordinary or unusual.

Years 1 and 2 follow the expected claim rate. In year 3, mortality shifts by the amount you choose. A real shift should push points toward and past the bands. A small one hides inside them.

Synthetic book of policies with a skewed face amount distribution. "Large" means the top 3% by face amount. Band width is the simulated standard deviation divided by the simulated mean, so it reads as a percentage of plan. A/E% is actual minus expected, as a percentage of expected.

Design choices worth explaining

  • The simulation runs once a year because it is expensive. Reporting runs on demand. Separating the two keeps the notebook fast.
  • Simulated spread is rescaled relative to plan, so the bands answer the question the business asks: how far can actual land from budget by chance alone?
  • Large policies are analyzed as their own segment, because one large claim can dominate a month. Try the segment control in the demo.

The simulation captures process variance, the randomness of whether a claim occurs. It does not capture parameter variance, the uncertainty in the assumptions themselves. Knowing what a model leaves out matters as much as knowing what it shows.

Result

  • About 75% less timespent on this analysis each year
  • One clickfrom simulation to charts, with no manual date edits
  • Feeds a monthly meetingon financial issues

What I learned

A good monitoring tool answers a decision-maker's question directly. The statistics were the easier part. Making them readable, self-updating, and honest about what they cover is what made the tool usable.

Project 3 · Weeks 5–9

Results analysis

Does mortality follow the seasons, and for whom?

My longest project had two phases. I delivered each as an interactive dashboard in Snowflake, built with Streamlit and Python.

Phase 1: RGA compared with the industry

The team wanted to know whether RGA's book shows the same seasonal pattern as the life insurance industry, and whether the pattern changed after COVID. I followed a 2012 RGA study of death benefit A/E by quarter and pulled industry data from Capital IQ. While reviewing the method, I found that expected values could be distorted by in-year growth. I refined the approach by expressing each quarter as a share of annual mortality, which removes that bias. A dashboard with two independent panels let the team compare methods and sets of years side by side. It also exposed a data timing issue that explained one surprising pattern: claims reported later than expected were landing in the following quarter.

Phase 2: insured compared with the general population

The second question was how insured mortality seasonality compares with the general population. I built a heatmap dashboard that sits alongside the existing Power BI visuals for the general population. Users filter by sex, age, year, and cause of death, and can grey out cells with too few deaths to trust, a common problem with insured data. The research was preliminary when I presented. I shared early observations as hypotheses for further study.

What I learned

  • A finding only has impact if the people who need it can explore it. The dashboards let the team test their own questions instead of reading a static report.
  • Check reporting lags before interpreting a seasonal pattern. A surprising quarter can be a timing artifact.
  • Small cells mislead. Cuts by age and cause leave some groups with very few deaths, so thin cells need to be flagged instead of read as signal.
  • Insured lives are underwritten, so differences from the general population reflect selection as well as seasonality.

Internal, industry, and population data come from different sources with different structure and granularity. Making them comparable took more effort than the analysis itself.

Takeaways

Across all three

What the internship taught me

A T-shaped skillset

The vertical stroke is deep expertise in your own area. The horizontal stroke is the ability to collaborate across actuarial functions and other departments. I came in with depth in math and data work, and the summer pushed me to build breadth in how RGA's functions connect: how treaty terms define an observation window, how claims monitoring feeds financial reporting, and how a study's assumptions flow into pricing and reserving. Depth gets the work done. Breadth tells you which work is worth doing.

Reaching out

I was hesitant at first, worried that reaching out would come across as insincere. I learned that when you are genuinely curious about what someone does, they notice and are glad to help. That led to the best experiences of my summer, including projects with people outside my team and shadowing in other actuarial functions I am interested in.

Working with AI as an actuary

On the conversion tool, AI sped up the mechanical translation, but the value came from a validation loop that checked every column against known output. Experience studies inform pricing and reserving, so an unverified shortcut is not acceptable. AI changes how fast the work moves. The accountability for getting it right stays with the actuary.

Habits I am taking with me

  • Understand the business rule before writing the code that implements it.
  • Validate against a known answer before trusting any output.
  • Design outputs for the person who has to make a decision with them.
  • Ask early, and ask people outside my immediate team.
